= The Future of the Software Industry
:author: Ryan Johnson
:description: Open-source editors alone cannot free us from platforms that hold our collaborators and data. I think the more interesting future is an industry paid for reliable service, not our inability to leave.

An open-source replacement for a subscription editor sounds like freedom until the rest of the team still works inside the old platform.
The files might move. The comments, permissions, shared assets, and people might not.
I think the future worth building is one where software companies can sell us reliable services without owning the conditions under which we can leave.

That is a different ambition from making a free version of an expensive application.
It asks what a software company is actually for once its customers have a meaningful choice.

== Replacing the editor is only the beginning

Imagine opening a design in a different editor.
The image looks right. The layers are there. The tools do what you need.
Then a colleague asks where to leave feedback, and the answer is still a link to the old service.

The replacement works. The departure does not.

This is the part of the open-source-versus-subscription argument that interests me most.
I am frustrated by the idea of paying indefinitely for permission to run a tool on hardware I already own.
But replacing the tool does not necessarily replace the arrangement around it.
If the platform is also where a team keeps its shared work, the decision to switch is no longer mine alone.

I can choose a different brush engine.
I cannot choose a different set of colleagues.

That gives the platform a reason to make collaboration inseparable from its own application.
The editor can become replaceable while the workspace becomes harder to leave.
A cheaper client does not solve that problem, and a beautifully written open-source client does not solve it by itself either.

== The network effect does not have to belong to one company

Social applications make the problem obvious.
An empty alternative can be better in almost every technical respect and still be useless to someone whose community is elsewhere.
A shared creative workspace can have the same shape: the value includes who is there and what they have already done together.

My first worry was that this would make the software industry's future even more concentrated.
If local applications become easier to replace, perhaps companies simply retreat into the parts we cannot reproduce independently: the network, the shared state, the accumulated relationships.

But that assumes the network has to remain inside a single company's boundary.
I do not want to grant that assumption.

An open-source platform could be hosted by one provider.
A federated platform could let independently operated services communicate.
An interchange format could let work move between tools.
Those are different properties, and none automatically supplies the others.
Publishing the server's source is not enough if moving away still means abandoning the working relationships stored in it.

The more demanding goal is for different providers to participate in the same useful system.
Changing the company that hosts a workspace should not require persuading every collaborator to change applications on the same afternoon.
Changing the editor should not mean starting the project's review history over.

That is not a claim that federation is easy, or that any existing protocol has solved all of this.
It is the condition I would want to judge a proposed alternative against.
Can I leave without making everyone else leave with me?

== What would we still pay for?

Suppose we get much further toward that arrangement.
The clients are open, the formats are usable across applications, and collaboration is not confined to one provider.
What remains for a software business to sell?

Quite a lot, I think. Just not the same kind of control.

I still want my work available from more than one device.
I want storage that survives a hardware failure, backups that can actually be restored, and a service that is awake when my own computer is not.
I may want more compute for a particular job than I can reasonably keep at home.
I do not want to operate a mini-data-center merely to avoid being trapped in somebody else's product.

Those are services, not failures of independence.
Paying someone to run them can be a perfectly good trade.
The important question is whether I can replace that someone without replacing my entire workflow.

[cols="1,2",options="header"]
|===
|What I want to keep choosing
|What I might pay a provider to do

|My applications and usable copies of my work
|Run reliable storage, backups, and synchronization

|Where a job executes
|Supply compute when my own hardware is not enough

|Who I communicate and collaborate with
|Host a service that can communicate with other providers

|Which identities and attestations I trust
|Manage keys and stand behind clearly defined verification

|How my work reaches people
|Deliver content quickly and help keep public services available
|===

This does not make subscriptions inherently bad.
An ongoing service has ongoing work behind it.
Neither does it make open-source applications costless to develop or maintain.
Good tools still need people working on them.

I am trying to distinguish paying for work from paying because the exit is blocked.
A company can provide the first without arranging the second.

== Trust is a service too, but it needs limits

Identity and authenticity were the other roles I kept coming back to.
If anyone can run the software or host a service, there is still a question of who is on the other end.
Someone may want a provider to manage their keys, help recover access, verify an identity, or stand behind a particular claim about a digital asset.

There is useful work there.
There is also an opportunity to build the next cage.

If every document is portable but one company is the only acceptable way to prove who created it, we have moved the dependency rather than removed it.
I would want room for different providers, different trust relationships, and people who manage their own identities where that makes sense.

And I would not confuse provenance with truth.
Evidence that a particular key signed a file does not, by itself, establish that everything in the file is true.
A useful guarantor has to say what it is guaranteeing, not sell a vague glow of authenticity.

Businesses might also pay for support, operational commitments, or contractual responsibility.
None of those requires a world in which the editor, the storage provider, and the identity provider must be the same company.
They might be bundled for convenience.
I want the bundle to be a choice.

== And yes, CDNs

After thinking through compute, storage, and identity, I remembered content delivery networks.
Of course those still have a job.

An open protocol does not put a copy of a large file near the person requesting it.
It does not provide bandwidth, operate distributed caches, or absorb an attack on a small public service.
Someone still has to run that infrastructure, whether the organization doing it is commercial, cooperative, or publicly supported.

This is where I resist turning an argument for decentralization into an obligation to self-host everything.
A small independent service should be able to buy delivery and protection without surrendering ownership of its application or community.
Using a CDN does not have to undo the freedom gained elsewhere.

But infrastructure can concentrate too.
An open application running on a choice of one practical provider is not the outcome I am hoping for.
The same question follows us down the stack: how much of the service can I replace, and what do I lose when I try?

== The commons still needs a way to get paid

Earlier in this line of thought, I had been arguing about domain names.
I was frustrated by useful names being held for speculative resale, and I started asking whether higher holding costs could discourage that behavior.
Then the question changed: if such a system collected money, why should all of it remain with the businesses collecting it?
Could some help fund the open-source infrastructure the rest of the web depends on?

I do not have a finished policy for that.
A rule meant to dislodge a speculator could also hurt a small builder who needs time to finish a project.
A funding pool needs governance, and a source of revenue is not proof that the money will reach the people maintaining useful software.
I would not treat a plausible loop on paper as a solved institution.

But the funding question belongs in this picture.
If open tools make the service market more competitive, the people maintaining those tools should not be an afterthought.
An industry built on a commons needs a deliberate way to sustain that commons, whether through paid maintenance, sponsorship, shared funding, or mechanisms we still need to work out.

Otherwise we are imagining a healthier market for hosting and leaving the toolmakers to figure it out alone.

== A future to choose, not a forecast to wait for

I do not expect every software company to collapse into a storage utility.
Craft still matters. So do good interfaces, integration, support, and the willingness to maintain something for years.
I also do not think incumbents will surrender a profitable captive ecosystem just because an open alternative is imaginable.

This is a direction I want, not an inevitable endpoint I can put a date on.

The stronger version of the open-source future is not simply that someone recreates an expensive application and gives it away.
It is that we make the surrounding services interchangeable enough for competition to remain possible.
The editor can improve without taking custody of the team.
The host can earn money without owning the files' future.
The identity provider can offer reassurance without becoming the only door through which a person is allowed to exist.

Then software companies still have plenty to do.
They have to make things people want, run services people rely on, and keep earning the relationship.

I want to pay a provider because it is doing a good job.
I want leaving to remain an option while it does.
